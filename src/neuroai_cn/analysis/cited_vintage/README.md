# Cited vintage: how old is the neuroscience that AI papers cite, and how old is the AI that neuroscience cites

In this directory I reproduce, on the local OpenAlex DuckDB mirror (snapshot 2026-03-30, read-only), three statements of the Transmitter NeuroAI article: the subfield mix of the neuroscience that AI papers cite, the shift in the vintage of that neuroscience between 2010-2019 and 2020-2025, and a reviewer's claim that machine-learning papers have a short citation memory. I wrote the definitions into `definitions.py` before I looked at the results. The tables are in `results/vintage/` and the tests in `tests/test_vintage.py` pin them. All counts are citation edges (one edge is one citing paper citing one work) in a mirror that holds works published 1990 or later.

Run order, one heavy job at a time: `python -m neuroai_cn.analysis.cited_vintage.build --stage labels`, then `--stage edges`, `--stage cross`, `--stage refage` (intermediate parquet under `DERIVED_DIR/vintage`, not tracked), then `python -m neuroai_cn.analysis.cited_vintage.vintage`, `.robustness` and `.reference_age`, and `python -m neuroai_cn.analysis.manifesto_citations` for the manifesto analysis. Under the machine load of the day the stages took about 18, 9, 7 and 7 minutes, and the three table steps a minute or two each. `--years LO HI --out DIR` recomputes a slice, which is what the heavy test does.

The citing side is AI papers by the extended classifier (subfield 1702 minus 26 topics, plus 1707, plus T10502) and, for the reverse direction, neuroscience papers (field 28), both on the primary topic. The cited side is "unrestricted" (primary topic in field 28) or "exclusive" (field 28 and none of the work's listed topics is an AI topic), which removes the AI papers that OpenAlex files under neuroscience only when their secondary topic reveals it. For the headline analysis I use every OpenAlex work type for the citing paper, as the flow figure does; article and review only is a sensitivity (restricting to those drops arXiv preprints). "Cognitive neuroscience" is the OpenAlex subfield of that name (2805) and "cellular and molecular" is subfield 2804; `subfield_breakdown.csv` gives all eight subfields. The share published "in 2005 or earlier" uses the boundary 2005, and I write the share up to 2004 beside it because 2004 is the boundary of a cut at "before 2005".

Claim 1, the subfield mix, is computed for AI papers under the 35-topic core definition, the definition behind the article's sentence. For AI papers of 2020-2025 citing exclusive neuroscience, 90.3 percent of citation edges go to cognitive neuroscience and 4.98 percent to cellular and molecular (unrestricted cited set: 88.3 and 4.3; 2020-2023 exclusive: 91.2 and 4.41). Under the extended classifier, which adds computer vision and neuromorphic hardware to the citing side, the shares are different (`subfield_breakdown.csv` holds both definitions), because the added AI papers cite more cellular and molecular work and more neurology, mostly the topic Brain Tumor Detection and Classification cited by medical-imaging papers.

Claim 2, the vintage shift, is computed for the extended classifier with the exclusive cited set. The median cited year moves from 2007 (2010-2019) to 2015 (2020-2025), the mean age rises from 9.2 to 10.1 years, and the share published in 2005 or earlier falls from 43.8 to 19.9 percent (up to 2004: 39.3 to 17.8). Unrestricted the median moves from 2007 to 2016 and the share from 42.5 to 18.0 percent. The 35-topic definition with the boundary at 2004 and the unrestricted set gives a median of 2008 to 2015 and a share up to 2004 of 34.95 to 14.96 percent, a replication check on the simpler variant. Restricting to 2020-2023, because 2024-2025 are unstable in this snapshot, gives a median of 2014 (exclusive) and a share of 23.1 percent. I kept citation edges to works dated after the citing paper (OpenAlex date errors) and count them in `n_future_dated` (about 1 percent in 2010-2019, 0.1 percent in 2020-2025).

My robustness check can fail, and it does, in part. When I remove 13,946 cited works (13,925 whose title matches the declared AI-method pattern, 11 clear and 10 borderline manual removals from the two top-25 lists; all listed in `removed_works.csv`) leaves the cognitive share within 0.7 points in the exclusive set but moves it by up to 2.2 points in the unrestricted set, which is over the 2-point threshold; it moves the median cited year of the recent period down by one year in most cells (two years for unrestricted 2020-2023), and the share published in 2005 or earlier of 2020-2025 up by 2.2 to 2.8 points, over the thresholds. Most of the works I removed are recent, so the recent period gets older after removal. The between-period shift is robust: the exclusive median shift stays at 8 years (7 for 2020-2023) and the fall in the share published in 2005 or earlier stays between 23.3 and 23.9 points at every stage (`robustness_shift.csv`). So the levels quoted for 2020-2025 are 1 year and 2 to 3 points softer than the baseline suggests, but the claim that the cited neuroscience got newer by about eight years does not depend on the mislabeled works. The primary-topic half of the rule is empty by construction: no field-28 work can have an AI primary topic under this classifier, and the count is zero.

The reverse direction is in the same tables (`vintage_summary.csv`, `vintage_by_year.csv`, direction `neuro_to_ai`). Neuroscience papers citing exclusive AI works cite older AI in 2010-2019 than AI papers cite neuroscience (median 2006, 47.6 percent published in 2005 or earlier) and the same vintage in 2020-2025 (median 2016, 17.4 percent; 2020-2023 median 2015). The reverse direction is the more recent one by 2025 (median cited year 2019 against 2017).

The reference-age test (`reference_age_by_field.csv`, `reference_age_pooled.csv`, `reference_list_coverage.csv`) measures all references that resolve to a dated work, for article and review citing papers. For AI papers of 2020-2023 the median reference age is 4 years, the mean 6.6, the 90th percentile 16 years, and 58.5 percent of references are five years old or younger (10 years or younger: 80.1 percent). For neuroscience papers of 2020-2023 the median is 8 years, the mean 9.7, the 90th percentile 21, and 36.2 percent are five years or younger (62.2 percent ten years or younger). The reviewer's spot check reported about two thirds at five years or younger and a median of 3 years for AI conference papers, and a median of 8 years for neuroscience journal papers; I reproduce the neuroscience median, and the AI median and share are close but somewhat older and lower than the spot check. Restricted to references that go to the other field, AI papers of 2020-2023 cite neuroscience with a median age of 7 years (43.4 percent five or younger) and neuroscience papers cite AI with a median of 7 years (41.9 percent). The 2024-2025 rows are provisional and flagged.

What I cannot show: the spot check was of conference papers and OpenAlex does not tell conference from journal reliably, so the AI rows mix both. Only 42 to 57 percent of AI works of 2020-2025 have any reference list in the mirror (52 percent in 2020, 46 percent in 2023, 42 percent in 2025; for article and review types 60 percent in 2020 and 71 percent in 2023), against 56 to 59 percent for neuroscience through 2024, so under-indexing of conference reference lists is a real possibility and the papers without lists are not sampled. Of the references that are listed, 80 percent of AI references and 91 percent of neuroscience references resolve to a dated work, because the mirror starts in 1990 and some cited works are missing; references to pre-1990 work cannot be aged, which understates the old tail for both fields and is larger for papers of the early 2000s (for the year 2000 every reference older than ten years is lost, which is why the 2000 rows are not informative). The comparison between fields is not affected equally, since the unresolved fraction is twice as large for AI. Median age is also sensitive to the growth rate of a literature: a field whose output grows fast has more recent work to cite.

Other limits. I classify by the primary topic only, and the share of works with any primary topic falls in 2024-2025, so the last two years are provisional. Subfield shares depend on OpenAlex's topic model, which labels a large part of "cognitive neuroscience" as psycholinguistics and cognitive science. The cited-work vintage uses `publication_year` as recorded, which for reprints and books can be much later than the idea. The `strict35` rows replicate the simpler 35-topic variant.

Files in `results/vintage/`: `matrix_cited_vintage.csv` (the count matrix every vintage table can be recomputed from), `vintage_summary.csv`, `subfield_breakdown.csv`, `vintage_by_year.csv`, `claims.json`, `robustness.csv`, `robustness_shift.csv`, `removed_works.csv`, `top25_inspection.csv` (the lists I read for the manual removals), `matrix_reference_age.csv`, `reference_age_by_field.csv`, `reference_age_pooled.csv`, `reference_list_coverage.csv`.
